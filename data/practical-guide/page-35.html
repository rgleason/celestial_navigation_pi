2. After Lunar Distancing, what was next? • After computing time (Greenwich Time,…GMT….UT/UTC), calculate Longitude. Calculate Latitude using a Meridian Transit or Polar sights. Re-evaluate chronometer’s error (number of min-sec fast or slow) for future sights.

3. How accurate was Lunars in the 1700s? • The Longitude Prize was awarded if you could demonstrate a solution better than .5° longitude accuracy. Harrison was the big winner. And Tobias Mayer won some prize money for his work on Lunars. The .5° was not the estimate for accuracy; it was a threshold or goal for the award. If statistical analysis is found to identify historic accuracy, that would be beneficial. • Ship Logs included Lunars, Altitude Sights, and DR positions. The ship logs are today’s evidence that Lunars was trusted. • For over 100 years after chronometers were introduced, navigators found it to be a valuable tool.

3b. How accurate is it Today? • With a good quality sextant, well adjusted, and used with skill, the goal for your Lunar Distance observation should be consistently +/- 0.2’ accuracy. The limitation is your skill given the stability of the vessel. It is easier on land.

4. Is Lunars relevant today? • Lunar gives the celestial navigator the opportunity to practice using their sextant. The more practice the better you become. Not only for Lunars, but for all sights. Practice makes perfect. Lunars can be done anywhere. At home in your backyard, near the water, on the water. You can do it during twilight, during the day, in the middle of night. Do it any time the Moon is visible, day or night. And of course, off-shore. And, it is not tethered to the internet. • Do you want to verify your sextant’s arc error and develop calibration data based on observation? Lunar method can help.
