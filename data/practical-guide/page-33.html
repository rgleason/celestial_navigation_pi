1. Lunar Distancing Objective and Background • Objective of Lunars was to determine TIME at a Baseline Meridian, Greenwich. The baseline time is now UT/UTC.

• Ancient Greeks and Babylonians theorized the Moon could be used for Universal Time and then observer could determine Longitude. In theory, anyone on Earth could tell the same event’s baseline meridian time by measuring the Moon’s “distance” to another celestial body. The “distance”, was an angle called Lunar Distance. And then after TIME was determined for the baseline meridian, Longitude could be calculated using the observer’s local time.

• 16th century mathematicians and astronomers advanced Spherical Triangles (and Navigator’s triangle) to the point where the math could be used to tell time by measuring the distance of the Moon to the Sun, planets, and stars.

• It takes the moon 27.32166 days to complete a full orbit around the earth respect to the background stars (Moon Sidereal month). Thus the Moon moves Eastward. Relative to the background celestial bodies, almost 13.2° per day. This translates to 32.94 arc-min per hr. or 0.55 arc-min per min. From that, 0.2 arc-min Marine Sextant measurement accuracy will be ~22 sec., and 0.1 arc-min measurement accuracy will be ~11 sec.

• You need a high quality Marine sextant to achieve .1’ measurement accuracy. At the equator, 4 seconds in time is 1 nm error. And 11 seconds in time is approximately ~3 nm error. That’s the limit for Lunar derived position accuracy a marine sextant.

• The maritime sextant was designed to meet the need to measure Lunar Distance. Double reflecting to measure 120° angles. Higher magnification to determine more precisely when the celestial body touches the Moon (today 7x are common). Shades over the Horizon mirror because you might be observing the Sun using the Horizon mirror. Eventually a vernier was added next to micrometer drum to more precisely distinguish 0.1 - 0.2 min. of arc.

• Meanwhile, mathematician and astronomers worked on the position of the celestial bodies problem. Isaac Newton theory of gravity was crucial to initially calculating the Moon and other celestial body positions. But, it took more time to figure it out with sufficient accuracy. In 1752-1762, Johann Tobias Mayer calculated Lunar Distance tables and altitude corrections. His widow was awarded, after his death, some Longitude Prize monies. In 1767, Nevil Maskelyne included Lunar Distance tables in the first Nautical Almanac.
